Methodology

Pre-registered before the experiment's first scored matchday. Any later change to these rules is logged in the public CHANGELOG and would be a reason to distrust the results — that's the point of publishing them now.

1. The question

Across the 2026 World Cup, who assigns the most accurate probabilities to match outcomes: large language models, the betting market, individual humans, or the human crowd average?

2. The forecast format

One unit of play per match for every forecaster: a probability triple (p_home, p_draw, p_away) for the 90-minute result, summing to 1.0. A draw is a valid outcome in knockout rounds too (this matches betting-market convention). No exact-score predictions are scored.

3. Freezing — the no-cheating mechanism

  • All forecasts for a matchday are collected and committed to a public git repository three hours before the day's first kickoff (models, market snapshot and humans share the same cutoff). The freeze script hard-aborts if it would ever run after kickoff.
  • A late prediction is a void prediction. Nothing is ever backfilled, for any forecaster, including the market.
  • Humans may resubmit until the freeze; only the latest pre-freeze submission counts.
  • Matches whose opponent is not yet decided (playoff placeholders) are not forecast by anyone until the fixture is real.

4. The AI protocol

  • Fixed lineup, frozen at launch: Gemini 2.5 Flash, Llama 3.3 70B, Gemma, GPT-OSS-120B, GPT-4o mini — all on free tiers. A model that loses its free tier after launch retires and is never replaced.
  • Identical prompt for every model and every match, temperature 0, with identical statistical context (Elo ratings, last-10 form, head-to-head, venue). No browsing, no live news.
  • Up to 2 retries on invalid JSON. If a model still fails, that match is recorded as failed — never replaced with a default. Raw responses are stored verbatim.
  • Parsed probabilities are clamped to [0.01, 0.98] and renormalised to sum to 1.0 (same rule for humans and the market's de-vigged odds).

5. Scoring

Multiclass Brier score per match, where y is the one-hot actual outcome:

Brier = (p_home − y_home)² + (p_draw − y_draw)² + (p_away − y_away)²
  • 0 = perfect · 0.667 = uniform (⅓,⅓,⅓) guess · 2 = certain and wrong.
  • Leaderboard metric: mean Brier over matches predicted, not over all matches.
  • Reference rows always shown: market, crowd (mean of human triples, computed at freeze), and uniform.
  • Abandoned/postponed matches are excluded entirely.

6. Qualification

To appear in the final rankings, a forecaster must have predicted at least 60% of the matches scored since their first submission. This lets people join mid-tournament without being punished for matches before they arrived, while preventing cherry-picking. Unqualified rows are shown greyed out.

This is about coverage since you joined, not raw pick count — which is why the leaderboard shows a fraction like 8 / 12. A worked example:

  • Someone who joins late, when only 12 matches have been scored since, and predicts 8 of them → 8/12 = 67% → qualifies with just 8 picks.
  • Someone who has been in since the start, with 38 matches scored, who predicted 22 → 22/38 = 58% → does not qualify, despite having far more picks.

So fewer picks can outrank more picks for qualification: what counts is the share of matches you could have predicted that you actually did.

7. Integrity & corrections

  • Scores are always recomputed from raw committed data; the scoring pipeline is idempotent and its output is never hand-edited.
  • Every data correction is logged publicly in the CHANGELOG.
  • The repository — code, every prediction, every result, every raw LLM response — is public. Verify the timeline yourself: each prediction file's commit timestamp must predate the kickoffs inside it.
  • The test suite (scoring math against hand-computed cases, a golden-file pipeline test, and a repo-wide data validator) runs in CI on every push.

8. What would falsify what

If the market beats all models and humans: calibration still belongs to the bookmakers. If the crowd beats its individual members: wisdom-of-crowds replicates here. If a free LLM beats the market: that is genuinely surprising and worth a closer look (and a check for leakage). Negative results are publishable results.
